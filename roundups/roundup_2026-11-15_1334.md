## Summary of the Week

The Nvidia–China espionage scandal dominated the week, escalating from an internal board investigation into a systemic governance and national security crisis: Jensen Huang acknowledged an affair with Alice Huang, an executive identified by US intelligence as a Chinese Ministry of State Security operative, and subsequently [resigned as CEO](https://www.politico.eu/article/jensen-huang-fall-exposes-eu-leaders-ai-nvidia-european-council/), triggering sharp declines in neocloud stocks and raising questions about whether billions in chip shipments to China circumvented export controls ([The Verge](https://www.theverge.com/ai-artificial-intelligence/1029562/nvidia-investigating-jensen-huang-relationship-chinese-spy), [Wired](https://www.wired.com/story/jensen-huangs-troubles-leaves-the-ai-industry-reeling/)). The enforcement gap in US semiconductor restrictions was independently confirmed when a former OpenAI researcher produced forensic evidence that DeepSeek, Moonshot AI, MiniMax, and Ant Group are running Nvidia's latest Blackwell GPUs despite export controls, with estimated hardware costs exceeding $500 million ([The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/former-openai-researcher-says-he-found-nvidia-blackwell-fingerprints-hidden-inside-six-chinese-ai-models/)). Against this backdrop, European policymakers convened their first dedicated AI summit in Dublin while simultaneously confronting their own strategic vulnerability: the EU has no homegrown alternative to Nvidia ([Politico EU](https://www.politico.eu/article/europe-needs-nvidia-spy-scandal-ai-microchips-germany-jensen-huang/)), and a major Microsoft outage renewed calls for digital sovereignty ([Politico EU](https://www.politico.eu/article/microsoft-outage-renews-european-calls-to-detox-from-us-tech/)). Regulatory frameworks continued to crystallise across jurisdictions—the EU's Digital Fairness Act named specific platforms, Australia proposed mandatory independent audits for frontier AI models, and Scott Wiener's election to Congress signalled an imminent tightening of US federal AI policy ([MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/11/09/1148182/nancy-pelosis-replacement-is-ready-to-regulate-ai/), [The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/australias-proposed-ai-safety-bill-would-force-frontier-model-makers-to-open-up-to-independent-testing/)).

## What Were Followed Accounts Discussing This Week?

Followed accounts—predominantly Gary Marcus, Yann LeCun, and a rotating cast of researchers and policymakers—converged on four recurring themes across the week, with the intensity and framing of each shifting as events developed.

**1. The Nvidia–Jensen Huang crisis: from scandal to systemic risk**

The crisis was the dominant thread from Sunday through Friday. Early in the week, commentary focused on the personal and reputational dimensions: Huang's prior engagement of a PR firm specialising in sex scandals ([Wired](https://www.wired.com/story/nvidia-ceo-jensen-huang-hired-a-pr-firm-known-for-handling-sex-scandals-last-year/)) and his positioning at what [Gary Marcus](https://x.com/GaryMarcus/status/2119782274954625273) called the "dead heart of the AI bubble." By mid-week, the framing had shifted decisively toward national security and market structure: allegations emerged that Huang had facilitated approximately $7.5 billion in chip shipments to China while undercounting sales to the government by 50,000 units, prompting calls for congressional subpoenas and scrutiny of Commerce Secretary Lutnick's relationship with Huang. By Thursday, following Huang's resignation, [Gary Marcus](https://x.com/GaryMarcus/status/2121087155531641246) was documenting cascading market effects—Nebius -21%, CoreWeave -14%—and drawing structural comparisons to the Cisco/dot-com collapse of May 2000. The Financial Times framing of Huang's fall as an indictment of "founder mode" governance was widely amplified.

**2. AI sector financial fragility and the Anthropic IPO**

A second persistent theme concerned the financial sustainability of the AI sector. [Gary Marcus](https://x.com/GaryMarcus/status/2119624303402418287) argued early in the week that semiconductor and cloud stocks were already down 40–50% and that "pure scaling is done." The Anthropic IPO—launching its roadshow the week of 9 November—served as a stress test for these concerns: early in the week it was beset by leaked prospectuses with incorrect financials and Goldman Sachs simultaneously restricting Anthropic's tools at its own firm ([Gary Marcus](https://x.com/GaryMarcus/status/2120025601340039399)); by Friday it was reported as [six times oversubscribed](https://the-decoder.com/anthropics-ipo-is-six-times-oversubscribed-and-on-track-to-become-the-largest-public-offering-in-history/), on track to be the largest public offering in history—a striking divergence between analyst scepticism and investor appetite. [Yann LeCun](https://x.com/ylecun/status/2120144057607315784) raised concerns about dangerous leverage from reliance on 2–3 centralised AI providers throughout the week.

**3. Export controls and the US–China technology competition**

Export control enforcement failures were discussed with increasing urgency across the week. Early posts focused on the Nvidia–China chip channel; by Wednesday, [Gary Marcus](https://x.com/GaryMarcus/status/2120721431990345811) and Senator Kennedy were calling for investigation into Commerce Department approvals of advanced chip sales. The theme broadened on Thursday with reporting that [US data brokers are selling approximately $500 million in reinforcement learning training environments to Chinese AI labs](https://the-decoder.com/us-data-brokers-are-selling-half-a-billion-dollars-in-rl-training-environments-to-chinese-ai-labs/), with export controls barely applicable. [Thomas Wolf](https://x.com/Thom_Wolf/status/2121344682495995959) and [Arthur Mensch](https://x.com/arthurmensch/status/2121271875829436860) reframed open-source AI as a geopolitical sovereignty tool in response, while [Arthur Mensch](https://x.com/arthurmensch/status/2121271875829436860) called for Europe to move with "greater speed, bolder financing, and tight coordination."

**4. AI capability claims and corporate accountability**

A thread running through the latter half of the week concerned the credibility of AI company claims. [Gary Marcus](https://x.com/GaryMarcus/status/2120856486096011752) led criticism of Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei's pre-IPO statements about near-term breakthroughs in medicine and biology, comparing him to Elizabeth Holmes; Eli Lilly Chair Dave Ricks disputed the disease-cure claims directly. Marcus also highlighted that AI coding agents require human cleanup on roughly 75% of tasks, challenging narratives of imminent autonomous systems. These concerns about misleading public statements ahead of a major capital raise have direct relevance to investor protection and market integrity.

### 2. Forensic evidence: Chinese AI labs running Nvidia Blackwell GPUs despite US export controls

Former OpenAI researcher Will DePue published the first technical evidence that DeepSeek, Moonshot AI, MiniMax, and Ant Group are using Nvidia's latest Blackwell GPUs in defiance of US export restrictions, reading characteristic chip signatures directly from published model weights. He estimates the hardware behind five training runs cost more than $500 million. Separately, US data brokers were reported to be selling approximately $500 million in reinforcement learning training environments to Chinese AI labs, with export controls barely applicable—compounding the picture of systemic enforcement failure.

CSET published analysis arguing that while the US leads in frontier models and computing power, China is pursuing a broader portfolio—robotics, embodied systems, brain-inspired architectures—that may prove resilient to narrow policy interventions targeting a single technology dimension. This structural framing is directly relevant to the adequacy of current US export control and AI governance strategies, which tend to treat the competition as a single race.

### 4. Nvidia chip tracking: private confirmation vs. public industry denial

Nvidia privately confirmed to US Representative Bill Foster that it would be technically feasible to implement chip-tracking anti-smuggling measures called for in the Chip Security Act, even while an industry group publicly claimed such measures were "potentially infeasible." This discrepancy is material to the policy debate on export control enforcement and has direct implications for the credibility of industry self-regulation arguments.
